## Words have jobs

Every word in a sentence has a **job**. English often shows the job with **word order**. Latin often shows the job with **endings**.

Before we decline <Latin>puella</Latin> or conjugate <Latin>amō</Latin>, we need names for those jobs: the **parts of speech**.

### The big idea

A <KeyTerm>part of speech</KeyTerm> is not “what the word means in the dictionary” alone — it is **what the word is doing in this sentence**.

<PosCards items="Noun ~ Person, place, thing, or idea|Verb ~ Action or state of being|Adjective ~ Describes a noun|Adverb ~ Describes a verb (or adj./adv.)|Preposition ~ Relates a noun to something else (in, with, to)|Conjunction ~ Joins words or clauses (and, but)|Pronoun ~ Stands in for a noun (he, she, it)" />

We will drill the first three carefully, then meet the others lightly.

### English examples

<ExampleSentence
  text="The **girl** carries **water**."
  gloss="girl, water = nouns · carries = verb"
/>

<ExampleSentence
  text="**Cold** water is in the **villa**."
  gloss="cold = adjective · is = verb · water, villa = nouns"
/>

### Why this matters for Latin

Latin endings are different for nouns and verbs. If you cannot tell “this is a noun job” vs “this is a verb job,” the endings will feel random.

<Callout kind="remember">
  Same English word can shift jobs: “I <strong>run</strong>” (verb) vs “a long <strong>run</strong>” (noun). Always ask: <strong>what is it doing here?</strong>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="culture" title="Grammatica">
  Roman schoolboys learned grammar as a system of word-classes and forms — the ancestor of the “parts of speech” lists you still use today.
</Callout>
