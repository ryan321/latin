## All five cases together

You now have a starter map of the five cases you will use constantly.

### Quick chart

| Case | Core question | Starter meaning |
|------|---------------|-----------------|
| **Nominative** | Who/what does or is? | Subject |
| **Genitive** | Whose? Of what? | Of / possession |
| **Dative** | To/for whom? | Indirect object |
| **Accusative** | Whom/what is acted on? | Direct object |
| **Ablative** | By/with/from? Where (with *in*)? | Means / place where |

### A reading habit

When you meet a Latin sentence soon:

<Steps steps="Find the verb|Find the subject (nominative)|Find the direct object (accusative) if any|Read prepositional phrases (in + ablative, etc.)|Put the meaning into natural English" />

### Next unit

**First declension** attaches real endings to these jobs: *puella, puellae, puellae, puellam, puellā…*

You will not be memorizing meanings of cases for the first time then — only the **forms**.

<Callout kind="try">
  Label the case ideas in English: “The girl gives water to the farmer in the villa.”  
  (girl nom · water acc · farmer dat · villa abl after in)
</Callout>

<Callout kind="culture" title="Why so many cases?">
  Latin is an <strong>inflected</strong> language: grammar lives in endings. That is a feature of many ancient Mediterranean languages — and it is why charts matter.
</Callout>
